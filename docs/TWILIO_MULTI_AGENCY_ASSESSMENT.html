Bottom line. Keep Twilio. Change the account model, not the vendor. One subaccount per agency, one voice adapter in the shared image, and the CRM as system of record. That is roughly ten working days of work, not a migration project.
01

The verdict

You integrated Twilio as a product feature inside one CRM. That instinct is right. The tenancy boundary is wrong. In a platform where every agency gets its own container, database and bucket, voice is still wired as a single-tenant service: one account, one set of credentials, one number pool, one webhook. It is invisible while Suzalink is the only caller. It becomes the first thing that breaks when agency number two starts dialling.

MOVE 01

One subaccount per agency

The Console creates it at provisioning. Master credentials never leave the Console; the agency container holds only its own subaccount key, auth token and TwiML app.

MOVE 02

One adapter, not per-agency code

A narrow lib/voice/ interface in the shared image. Twilio first, bring-your-own-carrier second. The agency fleet runs the same code, so a fix ships everywhere at once.

MOVE 03

Own the call, not just the button

Twilio is the carrier, not the system of record. Write the call row before dialling, keep the recording in the agency's MinIO, and reconcile anything the webhooks miss.

What I would not do

  • Do not migrate CPaaS to save money before fixing tenancy. Twilio is not your bottleneck, and a provider migration adds a second variable to a problem you have not measured yet.
  • Do not build a shared voice microservice yet. It centralises the blast radius, adds a single point of failure, and duplicates tenant knowledge the Console already owns.
  • Do not resell minutes without per-agency usage records. You cannot invoice, defend a dispute, or explain a spike you cannot attribute.
  • Do not rotate caller IDs to dodge spam labelling. In France that is precisely what the authentication rules target, and it makes the problem worse.
The one-sentence version. Your integration is a good single-tenant integration; the platform is multi-tenant. Move the account boundary, keep the code, and make the CRM own the call record and the recording — that is both the faster and the more reliable path.

Two details in the repository matter for what comes next. Second, docs/VOIP_SOFTPHONE_PLAN.md already specifies the right shape: a provider-agnostic lib/voip/ adapter, a short-lived client token, signed webhooks, a VoipCall lifecycle and recording ingestion. The Twilio adapter is not in this tree, but the plan around it is sound — this assessment builds on that plan rather than replacing it.

What the plan already gets right. Short-lived tokens, signature verification, idempotent events, a durable recording copy, server-side dial guards, and an instant fallback to the existing tel: path. Those are exactly the pieces that make voice reliable. The missing piece is the account boundary per agency.
03

How other agencies do it

There are four patterns in the market. They differ less in carrier than in where the account boundary sits — and that boundary decides who owns the number, the invoice, the recording and the incident.

A · Shared global account

STATUS QUO

One Twilio account, one credential set, one number pool, every agency calls through it. The integration is built once and works immediately for the first tenant.

The platform pays, the platform owns the numbers, and nothing distinguishes one agency's traffic from another's until someone asks for a report.

Fit: a single-brand product. Not fit: a fleet you bill per agency.
Recommended

B · Master account, subaccount per agency

SAAS PATTERN

Twilio's own model for platforms. Each agency is a subaccount with its own numbers, usage records, API keys, webhook configuration and recording settings. The master only provisions and pays.

You keep one commercial relationship, one invoice from Twilio, and full per-agency attribution underneath it.

Fit: your default. Cost: one extra provisioning step in the Console.

C · Bring your own carrier

BYOC

The agency connects its own Twilio, Telnyx or SIP trunk. Credentials are encrypted in the instance, numbers stay in the agency's name, and the minutes sit on the agency's bill.

You carry no telecom liability and no fraud exposure. You also carry every carrier-specific support ticket.

Fit: large agencies and enterprise deals. Ship it: after subaccounts, behind the same adapter.

D · Embed a white-label voice vendor

BUY

Aircall, Ringover, CloudTalk and similar vendors sell partner or white-label programmes with a ready softphone, numbers and compliance. Integration is usually a screen-pop and a click-to-call link.

Fastest commercial launch, least control: per-seat pricing, the vendor owns the number and the recording, and the deep CRM workflow stays shallow.

Fit: an agency that wants voice as a checkbox. Not fit: voice as your differentiator.

What mainstream CRMs actually do

Close has long shipped calling inside the product and runs it on Twilio. HubSpot and Pipedrive treat telephony as a connected app or a partner carrier rather than owning the network. Salesforce exposes an Open CTI layer that plugs into a carrier or Amazon Connect. The consistent pattern is the same everywhere: the CRM owns the workflow and the record; the carrier owns the media. Almost nobody hardcodes one global carrier account across tenants — precisely because the first per-tenant invoice request breaks it.

What this means for you. You are already at model A with a working product. Model B is a contained change to provisioning plus a credential split; model C is an option you can sell later through the same adapter. You do not need to choose between building and buying — you need to stop sharing one account.
04

Where it breaks first

Most "Twilio incidents" in a CRM are not Twilio incidents. They are application-level misses around a carrier that is doing its job. In a multi-agency setup these seven surface in roughly this order.

01

Billing and margin

Symptom: one Twilio invoice at month end, and no defensible way to say which agency burned which minutes, numbers or recordings. A dispute becomes a spreadsheet reconstruction.
Fix: subaccount usage records plus your own VoiceCall table; a nightly reconciliation job; a markup rule written down before the first resale.

02

Blast radius

Symptom: one leaked API key or browser token lets an attacker dial from every agency's numbers, and the fraud lands on the platform's bill and reputation.
Fix: subaccount-scoped keys only; short-lived access tokens; server-side destination allowlist; per-agency daily minute and spend caps with a hard stop; rotate on offboarding.

03

Number ownership

Symptom: an agency leaves and wants "its" number. The number sits in the platform's account, the contract is silent, and porting becomes a commercial negotiation.
Fix: decide ownership in the agency contract before the first number is bought; keep numbers in the agency's subaccount; collect agency KYC; define a port-out and a dormant-number release policy.

04

Webhook routing

Symptom: a shared endpoint infers the tenant from the dialled or calling number. One ambiguous match writes a call, a recording or a screen-pop into the wrong agency.
Fix: one webhook URL per agency on its own domain (the container already gives you this); validate the Twilio signature with that subaccount's auth token; store AccountSid on every row and reject a mismatch.

05

Concurrency and rate limits

Symptom: one agency's power-dialer campaign hits the account's concurrent-call ceiling and every other agency gets a busy signal or a failed setup.
Fix: per-agency concurrency budgets, a queue in front of the dial, and an alert when an agency approaches its share. Subaccounts also make the ceiling visible per tenant.

06

Compliance and data residency

Symptom: recordings of French prospects sit in a storage region nobody documented, under a DPA signed by the platform, while the agency believes the data is its own.
Fix: a per-agency DPA and retention policy; EU media edge; recordings copied to the agency's MinIO and served through a CRM proxy; a consent announcement switch per agency.

07

Support and forensics

Symptom: "the call dropped at 14:32" is untraceable because the Twilio console shows one account and the CRM kept no CallSid.
Fix: persist CallSid, status history and quality stats; a per-agency voice health page (token failures, setup rate, webhook lag, recording ingest failures); alerts on answer-rate drops per number.

ProvisioningConsole

One idempotent step added to the existing wizard: create the subaccount by a deterministic friendly name, buy or attach the agreed numbers, create one API key and one TwiML app, write the five instance variables, then call /api/instance/health and a new voice self-test. Retry-safe, like the thirteen steps already there.

Provider adapterlib/voice

A narrow interface: mintToken, verifyWebhook, parseEvent, fetchCall, downloadRecording, startBridge. Twilio is the first implementation; BYOC is a second credential set behind the same interface, not a second code path in the UI.

Token endpointAPI

Authenticated session, feature flag and seat check, then a one-hour Twilio Access Token with a VoiceGrant bound to the agency's TwiML app. The API secret never reaches the browser, and the token cannot outlive the shift.

Call creationAPI

Server-side only: re-run the existing guards (opt-out, recently contacted, shared phone, 24-hour pause), enforce the destination allowlist and spend cap, write the VoiceCall row, return the call id. The browser passes it to device.connect({ params: { callId } }) so the TwiML handler can attach the leg to the right record without guessing by phone number.

TwiML & webhooksAPI

TwiML returns a <Dial> with the agency's caller ID, dual-channel recording and status callbacks — or a <Conference> if supervision is in scope. The webhook validates X-Twilio-Signature against the public URL built from APP_URL, not the Host header, and is idempotent on CallSid plus a status rank.

RecordingWorker

On recordingStatusCallback, download once, store under the agency's MinIO key, write the key on the call row, and serve playback through an authorised CRM proxy. Twilio's URL is treated as a short-lived source, never as the archive. Retention and deletion follow the agency's policy.

ReconciliationCron

A sweeper closes calls stuck in dialling, ringing or answered for more than three hours, using the provider's call resource; a nightly job compares subaccount usage with the internal call table and flags gaps. This is what makes the system reliable when a webhook is lost, not retried, or delivered out of order.

The master account SID and auth token belong in the Console only. They are never injected into a tenant container, never bundled into the image, and never readable through the instance API.

Two details that cause most production bugs. Twilio request validation uses the account auth token, not the API key secret — storing only an API key leaves you unable to verify webhooks. And behind Traefik the request URL your framework sees is not always the public URL Twilio signed; reconstruct it from APP_URL plus path and query, or every signature check fails intermittently.

Supervision changes one decision now

If managers must listen, whisper or barge, the call has to live in a Twilio Conference rather than a simple <Dial>. Listen is a muted participant, whisper uses the participant's coach attribute, barge is an unmuted join. Each supervisor adds one billed leg and one audit row. Retrofitting conference onto a dial-based call flow is a rewrite of the call-control layer, so decide before phase one — not when the supervision screen is designed.

Why not a shared voice microservice

It is the obvious refactor, and it is the wrong first move. A single service holding every agency's carrier credentials becomes the highest-value target in the platform, a single point of failure for all calls, and a second place that must know which tenant a call belongs to. Your per-agency containers already provide the isolation for free. Put the shared logic in the image and revisit a central service only when number inventory or carrier contracts genuinely need one.

07

France is the constraint, not Twilio

Your own VoIP plan already names the hard part: SDRs in Algeria, France and Madagascar calling French prospects, with a caller ID that must work regardless of where the SDR sits. That is a numbering and authentication problem before it is a software problem.

RequirementWhat to verify in writingWhere it lands
French mobile caller ID (06/07) Arcep reserves 06/07 for interpersonal communication; business and automated traffic is pushed to 09. Twilio's French inventory is built mainly around geographic and non-geographic fixed numbers — do not assume mobile is available. Ask which allocation, which KYC, which paperwork. Provider choice; possibly a French operator behind an Elastic SIP Trunk, keeping one Twilio API.

Agents outside France Does the acceptable-use policy allow agents connecting from Algeria or Madagascar, with a French legal entity and French staff? Get a written answer, not a support thread. Provider contract; preflight test from each country.
Recording consent CNIL and the secrecy of correspondence generally require informing the callee. Your decision is to record without an announcement; keep it a per-agency switch and treat the legal call as the agency's, documented. Config switch, call announcement, DPA, retention.
Data residency Where the recording is stored by default, whether the region can be pinned to the EU, and what the DPA covers per subaccount. EU media edge, MinIO in the EU, per-agency DPA.
The decision that can invalidate the plan. If 06/07 mobile caller ID is non-negotiable and Twilio cannot provide it for agents outside France, no amount of application work fixes that. Resolve it with the carrier in writing before phase one. The adapter keeps that decision reversible; the number strategy does not.

There is a middle path worth pricing: keep Twilio for the API, SDK, webhooks and recording pipeline, and terminate the French leg through a French carrier over Elastic SIP Trunking. You get French numbers and CLI from an operator licensed for them, and one consistent application layer. It is more moving parts than a pure Twilio number, and less than a second voice stack.

08

What to ask Twilio before you build

Ten questions, one email. The answers decide the number plan and the contract; they should not decide the code, because the adapter is designed to absorb them.

  1. French 06/07: can you provision mobile French numbers on a subaccount for outbound caller ID, under which allocation, with what KYC and paperwork?
  2. Attestation: what attestation do those numbers carry, and what do French carriers display to the callee? Can you share real test calls to Orange, SFR and Bouygues?
  3. Remote agents: does your acceptable-use policy allow agents connecting from Algeria and Madagascar for a French legal entity? Any clause about the agent's location or IP?
  4. Recording region: where are recordings stored by default, can the region be pinned to the EU, and what is the retention and deletion model?
  5. DPA: can each subaccount sit under its own data processing agreement with the agency as controller, or only under the master?
  6. Subaccount operations: are per-subaccount API keys, usage records and webhook configuration fully supported at fleet scale? Any limit on subaccounts?
  7. Concurrency: what are the account and subaccount concurrency limits, and can they be raised per tenant?
  8. Portability: who is the legal holder of a number, and what is the port-in and port-out process when an agency leaves?
  9. SIP trunking: can we terminate French numbers from a local carrier over Elastic SIP Trunking while keeping your Voice SDK and webhooks? What changes in caller ID and attestation?
  10. Commercials: per-minute rates to French mobile and fixed, number fees, recording storage, and volume tiers for a fleet of agencies.

Ask for the answers that matter in writing, from the account team rather than support. The two that can change the architecture are 1 and 3; the rest are contract and cost details.

09

The decision

You do not have a Twilio problem. You have a boundary problem that Twilio makes visible. The integration works because one account is doing all the work; it stops working when the platform has to explain, bill, isolate and support that work per agency.

The reliable path is conservative: keep the vendor, move each agency into its own subaccount, put one adapter in the shared image, and make the CRM own the call and the recording. It is less work than a migration, it preserves the isolation model you already built everywhere else, and it turns voice from a hidden shared dependency into a provisioned, billable, supportable part of the product.

Next action. Create the Suzalink subaccount and split the credentials this week. Everything else — recording, supervision, BYOC, the Console step — can follow without reopening that decision.
